🇳🇴 Norway

⛰️ Bergen

The colorful wharf town wrapped by seven mountains, fjords, and old troll tales

📖 The Story

Long ago, on the west coast of Norway, sailors built a town tucked between seven tall mountains and the sea. They named it Bergen, and ships came and went along a deep, quiet harbor that never froze. From here, boats could reach hundreds of narrow blue fjords cutting deep into the land, so people still call Bergen "the gateway to the fjords."

About 700 years ago, merchants from faraway German towns sailed in and built rows of tall, pointy-roofed wooden houses right along the wharf. They called their little trading post the "Hanseatic Kontor," and they bought something Norway had plenty of: stockfish, cod that fishers caught by the ton and hung to dry in the wind until it turned hard as wood. Dried this way, a fish could last for years without spoiling, so ships carried it all the way to Germany, Portugal, and Italy.

Bergen's wooden wharf, called Bryggen, burned down again and again over the centuries, since fire loves old wooden houses packed close together. But each time, people rebuilt it exactly the same way, plank by plank, on the very same spots. That's why the crooked, leaning houses you see today still look almost exactly like they did hundreds of years ago.

Bergen is also famous for something else: rain! Clouds roll in from the sea and get stuck against the mountains, so it rains here more than almost anywhere else in Europe. Locals just shrug and say, "there's no bad weather, only bad clothes," and pull on a raincoat. And when the sky finally clears, you can ride a little red train up Mount Fløyen and see the whole colorful town, harbor, and fjords spread out below you like a map.

🤩 Did you know?

  • 1Bergen is called the "Gateway to the Fjords"—boats leave its harbor for some of Norway's most famous fjords.
  • 2The town is tucked between seven mountains, and a funicular train called Fløibanen has climbed one of them, Mount Fløyen, since 1918.
  • 3Bryggen, Bergen's old wooden wharf, is a UNESCO World Heritage Site—even though it has burned down and been rebuilt plank-for-plank many times.
  • 4Hundreds of years ago, German merchants traded here for stockfish—cod dried so hard in the wind it could last for years without going bad.
  • 5Bergen gets so much rain that locals keep an umbrella by the door all year round—it's often called the rainiest city in Europe.
  • 6A hill near Bergen is named Troldhaugen, "Hill of the Trolls," after old Norwegian legends about trolls hiding in the mountains.

📍 Places to visit

📍 Bryggen (Hanseatic Wharf)

These colorful, crooked wooden houses were once the Hanseatic merchants' trading post, packed with warehouses for stockfish and goods. Walk through the narrow wooden passageways and you're walking where sailors and traders hurried for 700 years.

📍 Fløibanen Funicular (Mount Fløyen)

Hop on this cheerful red funicular and in about 8 minutes you'll climb straight up Mount Fløyen. At the top, all of Bergen—rooftops, harbor, and fjords—spreads out below like a giant map.

📍 Fisketorget (Fish Market)

Right by the harbor, fishmongers have sold the day's catch for centuries, from shrimp to king crab. It's still the best place to smell the sea and see what Bergen's fishing boats brought home that morning.

📍 Troldhaugen

This hillside villa was home to the famous composer Edvard Grieg, who loved writing music looking out over the water. Its name means "Hill of the Trolls"—a nod to the old tales that trolls lived in Norway's misty mountains.
