🚋 Lisbon

Where explorers set sail from seven hills — and a quake taught the world to build safer

📖 The Story

Long ago, on the western edge of Europe, ships set sail from a hilly city called Lisbon to explore the whole wide world. Portuguese sailors were some of the first Europeans to cross oceans no one had mapped before, sailing all the way around Africa to India. Lisbon grew rich and dazzling, its harbor filled with ships loaded with spices, silk, and gold. People called it the gateway between Europe and the rest of the world.

Then, on a quiet morning in 1755, everything changed. It was a holiday, so almost everyone was inside churches when the ground suddenly began to shake — hard. Minutes later a giant wave rushed in from the sea, and fires raced through what was left standing. In just a few hours, one of Europe's grandest cities lay in ruins.

But Lisbon didn't give up. A clever leader named the Marquis of Pombal ordered brand-new streets built in neat, straight lines, and — this is the amazing part — the new buildings had a hidden wooden skeleton inside their walls that could wobble and bend instead of crashing down in the next earthquake. Builders even marched soldiers around tiny models to shake them like an earthquake, just to test the design! It became one of the world's first earthquake-proof cities.

Today Lisbon is full of color again. Little yellow trams squeak and rattle up and down its steep hills like slow-motion roller coasters, and a tower by the river still stands guard exactly as it did when sailors waved goodbye centuries ago. Bite into a warm custard tart baked from a secret monastery recipe, and you're tasting a sweetness that has delighted Lisbon for hundreds of years.

🤩 Did you know?

  • 1Lisbon is built on seven hills, so its cute yellow trams — like the famous Tram 28 — climb and dip through the streets like slow-motion roller coasters.
  • 2On November 1, 1755, a massive earthquake — followed by a giant wave and raging fires — destroyed most of the city in just a few hours. It's remembered as one of the deadliest disasters in European history.
  • 3After the quake, builders invented the 'Pombaline cage' — a flexible wooden frame hidden inside walls that lets buildings sway instead of collapse. It's one of the first earthquake-resistant building designs ever made.
  • 4To test the new design, workers marched troops of soldiers around a model building to shake it like a real earthquake — no computers, just marching feet!
  • 5Belém Tower and Jerónimos Monastery were built to celebrate Portugal's great ocean voyages. Because they stood a little outside the city center, both survived the 1755 earthquake almost untouched.
  • 6The sweet custard tarts called pastéis de Belém were first baked by monks at the monastery, and the exact recipe is still a closely guarded secret today.

📍 Places to visit

📍 Belém Tower

This fairy-tale tower was built right at the river's edge to guard Lisbon's harbor — and to wave farewell to sailors heading off to explore the world. Carved into its stone are ropes and other symbols of the sea, since ships and sailors were what made Portugal's great voyages possible.

📍 Jerónimos Monastery

King Manuel I paid for this huge, beautiful monastery with riches brought back from Vasco da Gama's voyage to India. Its stone columns are carved with ships, ropes, and sea creatures — and its monks are the very ones who first baked the famous custard tarts.

📍 São Jorge Castle

Perched on Lisbon's highest hill, this castle has watched over the city for more than a thousand years. Climb its walls and you can see all seven hills of Lisbon spread out below — plus peacocks strolling proudly around the grounds!

📍 Praça do Comércio (Commerce Square)

This grand riverside square was built after the 1755 earthquake as the shining centerpiece of Lisbon's brand-new, earthquake-safer downtown. A giant statue of a king on horseback stands where a royal palace once stood, before it was destroyed.
