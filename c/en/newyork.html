🗽 New York

The tiny trading town that grew into a city that touches the sky

📖 The Story

Long, long ago, before New York had a single skyscraper, it was a cluster of green islands where the Lenape people lived. Dutch sailors crossed the ocean and built a tiny trading town at the tip of one island, naming it New Amsterdam after their home city far away. Beavers, not subways, were the big business back then—their fur was worth a fortune in Europe!

A few decades later, English ships sailed into the harbor and took the town without firing a single shot. They renamed it New York, after an English duke, and the tiny trading post kept growing bigger and busier every single year. Ships from every corner of the world sailed in, bringing people who would call this city home.

By the early 1900s, New Yorkers dreamed of touching the sky. Two teams of builders raced each other to put up the tallest building on Earth, and the winner, the Empire State Building, shot up in about a year and a half—so fast the steel frame rose almost a floor a day! For nearly 40 years, nothing on the planet stood taller.

Yet the very same city that reaches for the clouds also carved out something completely different right in its middle: Central Park. Every hill, lake, and boulder in the park was placed there by hand—not one bit of it grew there naturally! Today you can ride a glass elevator to the top of a skyscraper, then walk straight into a hand-built forest, all without ever leaving one amazing city.

🤩 Did you know?

  • 1Dutch settlers are said to have 'bought' Manhattan Island from the Lenape for goods worth about 60 guilders—an old story people still tell, though the Lenape didn't think about land the way Europeans did.
  • 2New York used to be called New Amsterdam, then New York, then briefly New Orange, then New York again!
  • 3The Statue of Liberty was a gift from France, and her copper skin once shone reddish-brown like a new penny—now it's green after more than a century in salty sea air.
  • 4The Empire State Building went up in roughly 410 days, and workers sometimes finished more than four floors in a single week.
  • 5Central Park is completely man-made—every rock, tree, path, and lake was designed and built by people, not nature.
  • 6New York's subway never sleeps—it's one of the few systems in the world that runs trains 24 hours a day, every single day of the year.

📍 Places to visit

📍 Statue of Liberty

This giant copper lady was a gift from France, welcoming ships into New York Harbor since 1886. She once shone reddish-brown like a new penny before turning her famous green. Millions of newcomers caught their very first glimpse of America right here.

📍 Empire State Building

Once the tallest building on the planet, this skyscraper shot up in about a year and a half. Its needle-like spire was even designed to dock airships! On a clear day, you can see for miles and miles from its observation deck.

📍 Central Park

This giant green rectangle in the middle of the skyscrapers isn't natural at all—every hill, lake, and rock was designed and built by people. It took years of hauling in soil and planting trees to turn swampy land into today's woodland wonder.

📍 Brooklyn Bridge

When it opened in 1883, this bridge was the longest suspension bridge in the world, its huge stone towers rising higher than anything nearby. When the engineer who designed it fell ill, his wife Emily Roebling taught herself engineering and guided the workers until it was finished.
