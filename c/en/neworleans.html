🎷 New Orleans

Where jazz was born and a river bends like a smile

📖 The Story

About 300 years ago, French settlers paddled up the mighty Mississippi River and found a spot where the river curved like a giant smile. They built a town right there and named it after a duke back in France: New Orleans. Because of that curve, people still call it the "Crescent City" today.

Ships from France, Spain, Africa, and the Caribbean all landed on its docks, and each brought something new — spices, drums, languages, recipes. Cooks stirred it all together into one bubbling pot and invented gumbo, a stew so tasty it tastes like the whole world in one bowl.

On Sunday afternoons, in a place called Congo Square, people gathered to drum and dance and sing. Out of those rhythms grew a brand-new kind of music nobody had ever heard before — jazz! A trumpet-loving boy named Louis Armstrong grew up nearby and later made that joyful, wiggly music famous around the planet.

Here's the wildest part: New Orleans sits so low that parts of it are actually below the river, kept dry by walls called levees. Since the ground is too soggy for underground graves, the city buries people in little stone houses above the ground — so some call it a "city of the dead" hiding right next to a city full of life, music, and parades.

🤩 Did you know?

  • 1New Orleans is nicknamed the "Crescent City" because the Mississippi River curves around it like a crescent moon.
  • 2Parts of the city actually sit below sea level — giant walls called levees and pumps keep the river out.
  • 3Jazz was born here in the early 1900s, when drumming and dancing at Congo Square turned into a brand-new kind of music.
  • 4Since the ground is too wet for digging deep, many New Orleanians are buried above ground in little stone tomb-houses.
  • 5During Mardi Gras, parade floats toss out beads to the crowd, and a "king cake" is baked with a tiny plastic baby hidden inside.
  • 6The St. Charles streetcar has been clanging through the city since 1835 — one of the oldest streetcar lines still running anywhere in the world.

📍 Places to visit

📍 Jackson Square

The lively main square of the old French Quarter, ringed by artists, musicians, and horse-drawn carriages. It sits right where the first French settlers built their town beside the curving river.

📍 St. Louis Cathedral

The oldest continuously used Catholic cathedral in the United States, its tall white towers watch over the square. It has stood — and been rebuilt — since French and Spanish days.

📍 Preservation Hall

A small, plain-looking hall in the French Quarter where jazz musicians have played nightly for decades, close enough to touch. There are no fancy seats and no microphones needed — just the sound born in this very city, live and close.

📍 St. Louis Cemetery No. 1

One of the city's famous "cities of the dead," packed with above-ground stone tombs because the soggy ground made digging deep impossible. Walking its narrow lanes feels like wandering a tiny stone town.
